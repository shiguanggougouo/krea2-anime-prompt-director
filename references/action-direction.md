# Action Direction and Character Interaction

Use this guide whenever a character-led image needs movement, expressive body language, or interaction between two or more characters. The goal is a single readable illustration with a memorable story beat, not a list of pose adjectives or a description of an entire animation sequence.

## 1. Choose the beat that belongs in this scene

Start with what the character wants in this instant and what the environment makes possible. A rooftop invites a leap, landing, or glance over an edge; a crowded bathhouse corridor may call for a careful pivot while balancing a tray; a rain-slick tournament ring changes footing and throws water from each step. Match the action's scale and emotional energy to the location, weather, props, role, and canon personality. Do not default to a generic standing pose just because the user only supplied a character name. A quiet scene can still use specific body language, a hand gesture, an interrupted movement, or a meaningful gaze; preserve deliberate stillness when requested.

For one still image, select one instant: anticipation, the decisive action/contact, or follow-through. Do not narrate several consecutive movements as though they could all occupy one frame. Pick the instant with the clearest emotion and silhouette, then let earlier and later motion be implied by balance, trailing forms, and environmental response.

## 2. Describe a drawable pose instead of naming one

Build the pose from visible evidence. Select the details that matter for this action; do not mechanically list anatomy in every prompt.

- **Intent and expression:** what the character is trying to do, and the face or gaze that makes the intention legible.
- **Line of action and silhouette:** a clear curve, diagonal, compression, or counter-curve through the torso and limbs. Prefer an asymmetrical, readable shape over a square, front-facing stance.
- **Weight and support:** show which foot or hand bears weight, whether the body is planted, falling, airborne, bracing, or recovering, and how the center of mass sits over or beyond its support.
- **Mechanics and direction:** describe the turn, lean, bend, reach, push, pull, swing, dodge, or landing; include the relevant arm and leg placement, shoulder/hip rotation, and where the action is aimed. Give a prop a clear path and target.
- **Cause and visible result:** show contact or a clear near-miss, then one or two consequences that follow the force: cloth pulled taut, hair trailing, dust kicked outward, water displaced, a surface scuffed, or an object tipping. Effects must originate from the action and travel in a coherent direction.
- **Follow-through:** let hair, sleeves, ribbons, straps, and loose accessories lag behind the body in the same motion. Use overlap selectively; a frozen illustration should suggest momentum without turning into motion blur or visual clutter.

Use specific verbs and short mechanical clauses. “She springs from the wet stone, twists to clear the swinging beam, and lands with one palm braced against the wall” is more drawable than “dynamic, energetic, action pose.” Preserve the character's identity cues and keep the face, signature hair, costume, and important hands visible wherever the framing allows.

## 3. Make the setting participate in the movement

Treat the environment as a physical partner, not wallpaper. Let a wall, branch, railing, stair, loose stone, doorway, current, gust, or piece of furniture enable, interrupt, or redirect the action. Include a small, coherent reaction at the contact point and along the force direction. The setting should explain why this movement is happening here and make the moment more memorable.

Avoid generic debris, sparks, speed lines, or wind on every image. Use only effects that have a visible source, a direction, and a useful role in the composition. Keep cause and effect physically consistent: a landing compresses the knees and shifts gravel outward from the planted foot; a tray being caught tilts and sloshes toward the catch; a turning body pulls fabric behind the turn. Scale the reaction to the action so the character remains the focal point.

## 4. Stage interactions as a readable exchange

When two or more characters share the image, decide the relationship beat before writing their poses: who initiates, who responds, what passes between them, and how each feels about it. It may be combat, rescue, rivalry, teamwork, reassurance, surprise, or a playful interruption. Let each important figure perform a distinct role; do not give everyone the same stance or point all faces toward the viewer.

Make the relationship visible through several of these cues:

- **Spatial blocking:** who is foreground/midground, who faces or turns away from whom, their distance, height difference, and which way they can move.
- **Shared focus:** matching eyelines toward the other's face, hand, prop, or the incoming threat.
- **Contact or clear near-contact:** show exactly where hands, weapons, clothing, or carried objects meet, and which character causes the contact. If there is no touch, make the gap and intended target unmistakable.
- **Action and reaction:** give the initiator an observable action and the other person a physically and emotionally distinct response—brace, recoil, catch, reach back, yield, resist, turn, or change expression.
- **Consequences across bodies and space:** show the interaction changing balance, posture, clothing, a shared prop, or the environment. For combat, clarify the attack line and dodge/block/counter line; for cooperation, clarify how their gestures and weight support the shared task.
- **Readable staging:** separate overlapping silhouettes enough to identify limbs and faces. Use diagonals, depth, and selective foreground framing to connect them without tangling bodies or obscuring canon traits.

For three or more characters, establish one primary exchange and make supporting figures react to it in distinct, quieter ways. Do not give every character an equal action if that makes the focal beat unclear. For known characters, keep their story-specific forms, clothes, equipment, relationships, and personalities consistent with the chosen moment. If the relationship is uncertain, show a plausible visual exchange without inventing a canon event or claiming that it happened in the source story.

## 5. Compose one clear focal beat

Choose a camera angle that explains the body mechanics and the relationship. A low angle can strengthen a leap or looming opponent; a side view can clarify a chase or collision; a close shot can prioritize a handoff, catch, or expression. Avoid extreme foreshortening when it hides the action. Keep the main gesture readable at thumbnail size, separate key silhouettes from the background, and use the environment's lines to lead toward the contact point or face.

In the final prompt, combine the action into the natural-language paragraph rather than exposing a checklist. A useful order is: story beat and intent → decisive pose and force direction → partner's response/contact → environment reaction → camera/staging → small follow-through details. Use only the clauses that serve the image.

## Research basis

- Krea, [From sketch to anime panel with Krea 2](https://www.krea.ai/blog/from-sketch-to-anime-panel-with-krea-2): describes action panels in terms of motion, weight, and a clear focal beat.
- Krea, [Introducing Generative Sliders](https://www.krea.ai/blog/generative-sliders): explains that Movement shifts images toward dynamic composition, stronger camera angle, pose, and motion energy; suggests small Movement increases for cinematic and expressive character work.
- Walt Disney Animation, [Animation](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/animation/): identifies timing, clear staging, anticipation, follow-through, secondary action, anatomy, weight, and movement as principles that make performances feel alive.
- Walt Disney Animation, [Hand-Drawn Animation](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/hand-drawn-animation/): connects strong poses and expressions to a character's thoughts, and describes weight, mass, overlapping action, and full-body pantomime; its Moana 2 example notes precise action/reaction and eyeline coordination between characters.
- Walt Disney Animation, [Layout](https://www.disneyanimation.com/process/layout/): emphasizes staging, camera, dynamic composition, and making action and emotion clear through framing.

These principles are adapted here for single-frame prompt writing. They are not a claim that Krea 2 simulates animation physics or that one wording guarantees a specific result.
